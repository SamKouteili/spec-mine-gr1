\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

We evaluate \toolname on a suite of \GRone specifications drawn from
real reactive-synthesis benchmarks, comparing against the
state-of-the-art constrained LTL learner
ATLAS~\cite{ZhangCGD25} in two configurations.
All experiments run on a single core of an Apple~M-series machine
with a 10-minute timeout per mining task.

\subsection{Benchmarks}\label{sec:benchmarks}

We draw our benchmarks from the Spectra
project~\cite{MaozR21}, a curated repository of \GRone
specifications from the reactive-synthesis literature.
The full repository contains over~660 \texttt{.spectra} files across
10~subdirectories; however, most specifications use syntactic
extensions---enumerated types, integer variables, array indexing,
parameterized patterns, and macro definitions---that require a
booleanisation step beyond the scope of this work.
We restrict attention to the \emph{boolean-only} subsets in the
\texttt{bloemDebugging}~\cite{BloemJPPS12} and
\texttt{cimattiAnalyzing}~\cite{CimattiGMT20} directories,
which yield \textbf{60~parseable specifications} spanning
16--218~variables.
The benchmarks represent three families of
hardware-protocol specifications:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{AMBA AHB bus arbiter} (15~specs, 16--40~vars):
  models the ARM AHB arbitration protocol with request, grant,
  lock, and ready signals for 1--5 bus masters.
  Variants include guaranteed fairness, transaction constraints, and
  weakened assumptions.
\item \textbf{Generalized buffer} (12~specs, 24--97~vars):
  a producer--consumer buffer connecting 5--40~sources to
  receivers via request--acknowledge handshakes (GenBuf protocol).
\item \textbf{Parameterised arbiter} (33~specs, 22--218~vars):
  multi-master arbiters with 2--100~competing requesters (A2, A4, A5,
  G5, G20, and G100 families), with fairness and safety variants.
\end{itemize}

For each benchmark, we generate 25~positive and 25~negative lasso
traces using a mix of two strategies.
Half the traces in each class are produced by a \emph{SAT-based}
generator that constructs assumption-satisfying traces via Z3:
positive traces satisfy both assumptions and guarantees, while
negative traces satisfy the assumptions but violate at least one
guarantee.
The remaining traces are generated by \emph{random sampling} of lasso
traces of length~4--6.
This mixed strategy is critical: purely random traces often yield
trivial separators that do not capture the reactive
assume--guarantee structure, while purely SAT-based traces may
overfit to the generator's construction patterns.

\subsection{Baselines}\label{sec:baselines}

We compare \toolname against ATLAS~\cite{ZhangCGD25}, a recent
constrained LTL learner that encodes the mining problem as a
MaxSAT instance via the Alloy modelling language and
the AlloyMax solver.
ATLAS accepts user-specified structural constraints in the form of
Alloy predicates, allowing it to restrict the search space.
We evaluate two configurations:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{ATLAS[LTL]}: unconstrained LTL mining over the full
  operator set $\{\always, \eventually, \Next, \until, \land, \lor,
  \lnot, \rightarrow\}$ with no template or partition constraints.
  Mined formulas are synthesised via
  \texttt{ltlsynt}~\cite{MichaudC18} (Mealy machine output).
\item \textbf{ATLAS[GR1]}: we encode a \GRone template constraint
  and the environment--system variable partition as Alloy predicates
  (see Appendix~\ref{app:alloy}),
  restricting the root to an implication between
  $\always\eventually$ subformulas with appropriate literal
  restrictions.
  Mined formulas are synthesised via
  \texttt{slugs}~\cite{EhlersR16}.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
\toolname formulas are also synthesised via \texttt{slugs},
as they are natively in \GRone form.

\toolname solves 58 of 60~benchmarks (97\%) within the timeout,
including all benchmarks up to 218~variables.
ATLAS[LTL] solves 37/60 (62\%), while ATLAS[GR1]
solves only 25/60 (42\%).
The two \toolname timeouts are 56-variable GenBuf specifications
that require depth $D \ge 4$.
ATLAS[GR1] times out on all 16- and 22-variable benchmarks:
encoding the \GRone template as Alloy predicates
atop full LTL semantics proves prohibitively expensive at
these sizes.
At 34--40~variables, all three tools solve most benchmarks, but
\toolname and ATLAS[LTL] are faster than ATLAS[GR1]
(19--22\,s vs.\ 89--115\,s on average).
Beyond 56~variables, ATLAS[GR1] drops to 2/8~solved, and
at 76+~variables neither ATLAS configuration can find any
\GRone formula, while \toolname continues solving up to
218~variables in under 3~minutes.

Figure~\ref{fig:cactus} shows a cactus plot of cumulative benchmarks
solved as a function of wall-clock time.
\toolname is slower than ATLAS[LTL] on the 24-variable
benchmarks (G5 and GenBuf-5 families), where ATLAS finds small
formulas in 5--10\,s compared to \toolname's 10--25\,s.
However, \toolname continues solving through the harder benchmarks
while ATLAS[LTL] stalls at 37~solved and ATLAS[GR1] at~25.
Figure~\ref{fig:scatter} gives a per-benchmark comparison: most
points lie above the diagonal, indicating ATLAS is slower or
timed out.

The ATLAS[LTL] formulas are flat LTL expressions
outside the \GRone fragment.
Many are \emph{trivial}: $\eventually x_i$ (``eventually $x_i$ is
true''), $x_i \rightarrow x_j$, or simple disjunctions.
Several are \emph{tautologies} of the form
$\always(\Next(x \rightarrow x))$---a formula true on every trace
(see Appendix~\ref{app:detailed}).
None captures the assume--guarantee structure of the underlying
specification.

By contrast, \toolname produces formulas in canonical \GRone form
with justice conditions, safety invariants, and initial conditions
on both the assumption and guarantee sides.
On the 25~benchmarks where both \toolname and ATLAS[GR1] produce
results, the formulas are structurally similar---often agreeing
on the justice and guarantee variables---but \toolname tends to
discover richer templates with multiple justice conditions and
safety components, while ATLAS[GR1] is restricted to a
single-justice, single-guarantee template imposed by its Alloy
constraint (see Appendix~\ref{app:detailed}).

Since the purpose of mining \GRone specifications is to enable
reactive synthesis, we evaluate the quality of the mined formulas
by synthesising controllers.
All 58~\toolname formulas are realisable (100\%);
all 25~ATLAS[GR1] formulas are realisable;
but only 30 of 37~ATLAS[LTL] formulas are realisable (81\%).
The unrealisable ATLAS[LTL] formulas are predominantly trivial
($\eventually x_i$, $x_i \until x_j$) that happen to be
unsatisfiable by any reactive strategy.
